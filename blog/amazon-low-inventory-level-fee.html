Amazon Low-Inventory-Level Fee Explained: The Fee Nobody Saw Coming

Sharon Inventory & Fees Sep 4, 2026 7 min read

Amazon used to charge you for storing too much. Since April 2024, it can also charge you for storing too little. The low-inventory-level fee applies to standard-size FBA products when their inventory drops below roughly 28 days of supply relative to historical sales — and because the trigger is based on your own trailing sales history, it punishes exactly the sellers who run lean on purpose, sell through a spike, or restock "just in time." In 2026 the fee got sharper: it's now assessed at the FNSKU level instead of the parent ASIN, so a single thin variant can trigger charges. Here's how the mechanics actually work, who's exempt, and the restock rhythm that keeps this fee at zero.

What the Fee Is and Why It Exists

The stated logic is capacity: Amazon's network plans storage and staffing around expected inventory. When a SKU suddenly runs far below its historical demand pattern, the fulfillment centers absorbed the cost of the surprise — so Amazon priced it back to the seller. The fee is charged per unit sold during the period the SKU is below the supply threshold, and it's scaled by how far below the target your inventory sits.

The trigger isn't a fixed unit count. Amazon looks at your SKU's historical weeks of supply — a blend of the trailing 90-day and 30-day averages — and compares it against your current inventory. Fall under roughly four weeks of supply and the per-unit fee starts accruing on each sale.

Situation Fee applies? Why
Standard-size SKU, <~28 days of supply Yes Core trigger
New SKU / new parent (first ~26 weeks) No No sales history to measure against yet
Grocery products No Exempt category (2026 update)
Very slow-moving ASINs Generally no Exempt, but delivery promises may slow
Oversize products No Standard-size only

The 2026 Change That Bit Variations Sellers

The most consequential 2026 update (effective January 15) moved the fee from the parent ASIN level to the FNSKU level. Previously, a healthy parent listing could absorb one thin variant. Now each child SKU stands on its own: sell through your black color while the rest of the size run sits fine, and the black FNSKU alone can start generating fees — even though the listing looks well-stocked in aggregate.

Practical consequences:

  • Reorder by variant, not by listing. A blended "total units" view hides the SKU that's about to trigger fees.
  • Watch your fastest variant. The best-seller in a variation family hits 28 days of supply first — it is always your canary.
  • Rebalance inventory across variants rather than letting hot children starve while slow ones squat on cube.

The irony to manage: this fee punishes under-stocking while the aged-inventory surcharge punishes over-stocking — and the aged tiers got more expensive in 2026 ($0.30/unit/month minimum for 12–15 month stock, a new $0.35 tier beyond that). The profitable zone is narrower than it used to be: roughly 4–8 weeks of supply, restocked on a rhythm, not in heroic swings.

How to Avoid the Fee Without Hoarding Inventory

  1. Set a hard reorder point at 5 weeks of supply. Compute daily velocity per FNSKU (units ÷ 90), multiply by 35 days, and treat that number as your floor. At 4 weeks you're already in trigger territory — the alarm has to ring earlier than the fee does.
  2. Shorten your replenishment cycle before deepening your inventory. Two smaller POs landing three weeks apart beat one giant PO: same total stock over time, far less cube sitting at the back end of the cycle aging toward surcharges.
  3. Factor your inbound lead time into the reorder point. A 35-day factory-to-FC pipeline on a 30-day-cover product means you needed to reorder before the product even launched. If lead time exceeds target cover, the fix isn't bolder reordering — it's negotiating shorter lead times or better supplier terms.
  4. Use AWD for automatic replenishment. SKUs using Amazon Warehousing & Distribution auto-replenishment (at least 70% auto-replenished, no caps set) are exempt from the low-inventory-level fee — Amazon manages the floor for you, in exchange for AWD's own rates.
  5. Audit the fee monthly. It appears on your settlement report and the FBA storage fee report. A low-inventory fee line that keeps recurring on one SKU is a restock-process failure, not an Amazon problem.

Running Lean "On Purpose": Do the Math

Some sellers consciously accept the fee as the cost of minimal inventory exposure — a valid strategy for volatile or seasonal products. But make it a decision, not an accident. Compare, for a typical month of thin coverage:

  • Cost of the fee: per-unit charge on every sale while below threshold — effectively a few points of margin on that SKU's sales.
  • Cost of the alternative: one extra week of stock = one extra week of storage ($0.78/cu ft non-peak for standard-size) plus the cash tied up in COGS.

For most standard-size products, the extra week of storage costs less than the fee does. The fee is designed to be more annoying than the cure — which is why "order a week earlier" fixes most cases outright.

The Bottom Line

The low-inventory-level fee flipped inventory management on Amazon: the old "lean and fast" playbook now carries its own tax, and the 2026 FNSKU-level change means variation sellers can't hide behind a healthy parent listing. The winning rhythm is boring and deliberate — per-variant reorder points at 5 weeks, shorter replenishment cycles, monthly fee audits, and AWD where it fits. Run too lean and you pay per sale; run too deep and the aged-inventory surcharge waits on the other end. The margin lives in the middle.
